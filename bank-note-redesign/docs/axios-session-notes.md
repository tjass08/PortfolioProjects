Axios Speaker: Writing for Short Attention Spans
⭐ Biggest takeaway
People don't necessarily need less information. They need information that gets to the point faster.
The Axios speaker emphasized that readers are skimming more than ever, so the goal is to make the most important information immediately clear.
One of the biggest questions Axios asks when writing a story is:
What is the gist, and how does this impact me?

They try to answer both questions within the first two paragraphs.
What Axios does differently
1. Lead with "Why it matters"
Axios doesn't just tell readers what happened. They quickly explain why the reader should care.
For example, when Salt Lake City received its first Michelin star, the story wasn't simply:
Salt Lake City received its first Michelin star.

It quickly moved into what that could mean for restaurants, competition and the dining experience.
The lesson: Don't make the reader figure out why something matters.
For IBA content, this could mean moving beyond:
The SBA issued new guidance regarding XYZ.

And getting quickly to:
Why it matters: The new guidance could affect how banks handle XYZ.

That's especially relevant for Bank Note, where readers may be scanning several different stories.
2. "Selection, not compression"
This was probably one of my favorite concepts from the presentation.
When creating a newsletter, Axios doesn't necessarily write a giant story and then try to cut it down.
Instead, the approach is:
Find the best nuggets first.
Ask:
- What's the most interesting point?
- What's the most surprising statistic?
- What's the information the reader actually needs?
- What's the one thing I want them to remember?
Then build the newsletter around those pieces.
For us
Rather than taking a long regulatory update and trying to cram the entire thing into Bank Note, identify the 2-3 things bankers actually need to know and make those the focus.
3. Write like you're explaining it to a friend
The speaker recommended imagining you're explaining complicated information to a friend, your mom or someone over the phone.
That forces you to use more natural language.
Instead of:
Due to the fact that the company experienced a significant decline...

Just say:
The company lost revenue.

The speaker's overall writing philosophy was basically:
If you can say it shorter, say it shorter.
4. Break up complicated information
The speaker admitted this is something she struggles with herself.
When something is complicated, it's easy to write one massive sentence or paragraph trying to explain everything at once.
Her solution:
Break it apart.
Shorter sentences.
Shorter paragraphs.
One idea at a time.
This is particularly important for financial and regulatory content because the subject matter is already complicated. The writing doesn't need to make it harder.
5. Put strong words in strong places
One of her writing tips was to put powerful words at the beginning or end of a sentence.
She used the example:
"The queen, my word, is dead."

"Queen" is immediately important and "dead" gives the sentence a strong ending.
The broader point is that word placement matters.
Don't bury your strongest information in the middle of a paragraph.
6. Get rid of unnecessary adverbs and extra words
She gave several examples of replacing wordy constructions with stronger verbs.
Instead of:
She walked quickly across the room.

Use:
She hurried across the room.

Instead of:
He said angrily.

Use:
He snapped.

The idea is to find stronger, more specific words rather than adding more words.
📰 Axios newsletters
This was probably the most directly applicable part to our team.
The speaker said a typical Axios Local newsletter is around 900 words, but can contain 5-8 stories and is designed to be roughly a three-minute read.
The newsletter format is intentionally built for people who are skimming.
They also structure the newsletter intentionally.
They generally:
Start with harder/serious news → gradually get lighter → finish with something positive or fun.
They call that final piece their "happy card."
The reasoning is simple:
Don't leave readers with something depressing or stressful as the last thing they see.
They want the reader to finish the newsletter feeling good about it.
👀 Visuals matter
Axios uses a team of illustrators to help break up text-heavy newsletters.
The speaker said visuals are a big part of making the newsletter appealing, especially when there's a lot of information.
She also talked about emojis.
Axios uses emojis in some newsletters as a way to:
- Draw the reader's eye
- Break up blocks of text
- Replace traditional bullet points
- Add personality
- Make certain sections easier to scan
But she emphasized that context matters.
They wouldn't use a fun emoji next to a murder trial or serious tragedy.
For a financial newsletter, that means emojis could work in the right places, but they don't need to be everywhere.
📌 Recurring features can make newsletters more interesting
Axios uses recurring features that readers can recognize from week to week.
For example, their local newsletters might have recurring restaurant recommendations, weekend guides or other features.
The benefit is that you have reliable content readers can come to expect.
Possible IBA application
This could be something worth thinking about for Bank Note.
Instead of every section being strictly:
Headline → paragraph → link
there could occasionally be recognizable recurring elements such as:
- By the Numbers
- Why It Matters
- What Bankers Should Know
- Coming Up
- Quick Hits
- ICYMI
- The Week Ahead
We already do some of this with things like "Why You Should Attend" and "Your Team's Next Steps."
The Axios approach reinforces that these little recurring labels can make a newsletter much easier to scan.
📊 "Talker" stories perform well
When asked what generates the most traffic, the speaker said stories that are "talkers" tend to perform well.
Her definition was essentially:
"I can't wait to tell my friend about this."

Those stories tend to be:
- Trending
- Surprising
- Interesting
- Highly relevant
- Something people have an opinion about
- Something people naturally want to discuss
For IBA
That's a useful question to ask when deciding what deserves prominence:
"Would a banker actually bring this up with another banker?"
If yes, that may be a stronger lead story than something technically important but less interesting.
📱 Social media + AI
The speaker said Axios is increasingly seeing people discover news through social media.
Her team uses an AI tool called Marfeel to turn an article into an initial Instagram post.
The process takes roughly 30 seconds, although she still edits the AI-generated content before publishing.
She described AI as extremely useful for idea generation and research assistance.
For example, if she receives a 300-page city budget, she can use AI to identify potentially interesting data points and story ideas rather than manually reading every page looking for them.
But she emphasized that she still checks the original source and verifies the information.
Other ways she uses AI
- Finding alternative wording/synonyms
- Generating story ideas
- Identifying interesting data points
- Planning future coverage
- Getting a starting point for social posts
The takeaway isn't "let AI write the story."
It's more:
Use AI to get through the boring/tedious parts faster so you can spend more time doing the actual journalism and writing.
🎯 Know your audience
One of the speaker's strongest points was that writers need to actually talk to their audience.
She said she will have coffee or lunch with people who represent her audience and ask:
"What do you want from this newsletter?"
and
"What would make this essential to you?"
That feedback helps shape what they cover and how they present it.
That's a good reminder for IBA because our audience is very specific.
The question isn't simply:
"Is this important?"
It's:
"Is this important to an Iowa banker, and why?"
⭐ Practical takeaways for our team
If I had to boil the entire presentation down to things we could actually use, I'd say:
1. Answer "Why does this matter?" immediately.
Don't make readers dig for the relevance.
2. Put the best information first.
Assume people are skimming.
3. Select, don't just compress.
Choose the strongest nuggets instead of trying to include everything.
4. Make complicated things simple.
Short sentences and short paragraphs are especially valuable for regulatory/financial topics.
5. Write conversationally.
Imagine explaining the topic to a friend rather than writing a formal document.
6. Break up walls of text.
Headings, visuals, links, bullets, bold text and other formatting can make information easier to navigate.
7. Consider recurring newsletter features.
Readers can learn what those sections mean and quickly find what interests them.
8. End on something positive when appropriate.
The "happy card" concept could be useful for Bank Note or other newsletters.
9. Use AI as a tool, not the writer.
Research, brainstorming, summarizing huge documents and generating starting points can save significant time, but the human still needs to verify and shape the content.
10. Ask what the audience actually wants.
The best newsletter isn't necessarily the one with the most information. It's the one readers find useful enough to keep opening.
💡 The big connection to the other speakers
I actually think this Axios presentation fits really well with the storytelling presentation from earlier.
The storytelling speaker's message was essentially:
Don't just give people information. Give the information meaning.
Axios is taking a similar idea from the opposite direction:
Don't just give people information. Make it immediately clear what the information means to them.
So a pretty good overall takeaway from both sessions could be:
Data gets attention. Stories create meaning. Good writing makes both easy to understand.

That feels like a pretty damn useful theme for our marketing/communications team.
